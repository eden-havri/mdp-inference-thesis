\DocSection{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Planning in a stochastic environment requires two conceptually separate
operations. First, a policy is evaluated by averaging its return over the
environment's transition randomness. Second, a decision rule selects among
policies. Conflating these operations changes the objective: exponentiating a
single realized return before averaging is generally not equivalent to
exponentiating the expected return.

This manuscript develops a policy-inference formulation in which the target is
defined before an approximation algorithm is chosen.  The target assigns mass
to complete deterministic policies according to expected return.  Its modes
are therefore expected-return maximizers, while a finite inverse temperature
retains a distribution over alternatives.  The construction is a generalized
Gibbs posterior over decisions, not a posterior belief about unknown transition
parameters.  Related free-utility formulations also trade expected utility
against information cost \citep{ortegaBraun2011}, and recent general Bayesian
policy learning updates distributions over decision rules from a loss
\citep{kato2026}.  Our setting differs by targeting finite-horizon MDP policies
and by making the expected-return order of operations explicit.

Control-as-inference methods provide a broad connection between optimal
control and probabilistic inference \citep{levine2018}.  In stochastic
dynamics, however, the choice of graphical model and optimality likelihood
determines whether the resulting objective is risk-neutral, risk-sensitive, or
maximum-entropy.  We therefore do not treat ``inference'' as sufficient to
identify the control objective.

Expected-return Gibbs policy search and Metropolis--Hastings inference over
policies have direct precedent in \citet{wingateEtAl2011}, who also discuss
replica exchange. Neither the exponential target, its mode preservation, nor
policy-space MCMC is claimed here as a new inference principle. The research
question is how to approximate this target at a defensible computational cost,
and what control behavior follows from committing to a sampled policy. The
current practical candidate uses direct variational learning; corrected MCMC
and fixed-bank SMC provide separately assessed alternatives. Preliminary
development comparisons motivate, but do not replace, held-out validation.

The study has five components:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item It specifies an expected-return Gibbs measure over complete policies,
        including its reference measure, temperature, and execution semantics.
  \item It gives exact finite-policy oracles and variance-sensitive diagnostics
        that detect substitution of an exponentiated-trajectory objective.
  \item It implements direct policy-space variational learning with an unbiased
        masked score-function gradient, independent baseline, and exact
        policy-table entropy term.
  \item It separates variational family error, fixed-tape approximation, and
        MCMC mixing, retaining corrected guided MCMC and replicated SMC as
        diagnostic alternatives rather than conflating their guarantees.
  \item It defines a fail-fast comparison against five published algorithmic
        families with both policy-commitment and native execution semantics,
        strict total interaction caps, and separately labeled exploration aids.
\end{enumerate}

Exact small-instance checks validate target definitions, gradient expectations,
and sampling kernels before scaling. They do not establish full posterior
agreement for the variational family. The protocol is specified before
confirmatory results; this draft labels development findings as preliminary.
