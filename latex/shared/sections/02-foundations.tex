\DocSubsection{Finite-horizon stochastic control}

Let an episodic Markov decision process be
\(\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},T,R,\rho_0,H)\), where
\(T(\cdot\mid s,a)\) is a transition kernel, \(R(s,a,s')\) is a bounded
reward, \(\rho_0\) is the initial-state distribution, and \(H\) is the rollout
horizon. Time-to-go is included in the state whenever a stationary
representation would otherwise exclude an optimal finite-horizon policy.

For a deterministic policy \(\policy\in\PolicySpace\), a trajectory
\(\tau=(s_1,a_1,\ldots,s_{H+1})\) is generated by
\[
  s_1\sim\rho_0,\qquad
  a_t=\policy(s_t),\qquad
  s_{t+1}\sim T(\cdot\mid s_t,a_t).
\]
Its truncated return and the policy value are
\begin{equation}
  \Return_H(\tau)=\sum_{t=1}^{H}R(s_t,a_t,s_{t+1}),
  \qquad
  \ExpectedReturn_H(\policy)=\E_{\tau\sim p(\cdot\mid\policy)}
  [\Return_H(\tau)].
  \label{eq:return-value}
\end{equation}

\DocSubsection{Policy-level randomness}

A distribution over deterministic policies is different from a Markov
stochastic policy. Sampling one deterministic policy at the start of an episode
creates correlations across states and revisits. Sampling a new action from a
statewise marginal on every visit removes those correlations. Both execution
rules can be studied, but they must not be treated as interchangeable.

\begin{assumption}[Reference measure]
The deterministic policy class has a specified probability measure
\(\mu\). For a finite tabular class, \(\mu\) may be uniform. For a structured
or continuous class, the choice of \(\mu\) is part of the model and must be
reported.
\end{assumption}
